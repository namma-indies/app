# Namma Indies reading list

A short, curated starting point for IndieDex. This is a guide to published research, not a claim that one city's results apply across India. Entries link to the publisher by DOI. Where we have only a citation or abstract, that is noted. Add papers after checking the original source; keep the date, study setting, method and limitations with each entry. No individual dog locations or contributor data belong here.

## Population estimates, ABC and vaccination

- **Totton et al. (2010), [Stray dog population demographics in Jodhpur, India following a population control/rabies vaccination program](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2010.07.009)**. Counts before and after an ABC programme across five Jodhpur areas, plus a model of longer-term change. Useful for thinking about baseline counts, sterilisation and vaccination coverage, and repeated surveys. *The 13–18-year projected reduction is a model, not an observed outcome. Abstract checked; full text not in our library.*
- **Belsare and Vanak (2020), [Modelling the challenges of managing free-ranging dog populations](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-75828-6)**. An agent-based model tests ABC effort against immigration, dog catchability and vaccination coverage. Useful for deciding what a population monitor must measure before anyone calls an intervention successful. *Simulation, not evidence that any particular city's ABC programme did or did not work. Full text checked.*
- **Tiwari et al. (2018), [A Comparative Study of Enumeration Techniques for Free-Roaming Dogs in Rural Baramati, District Pune, India](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2018.00104)**. Compare ways to count free-roaming dogs and what each demands in the field. Useful when designing repeatable IndieDex population estimates. *The comparison used one village and dog catchability varies; do not transfer its preferred method untested to an urban map. Publisher abstract checked.*
- **Paul et al. (2016), [High early life mortality in free-ranging dogs is largely influenced by humans](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep19641)**. Five years of pup observations at seven sites in West Bengal. Useful for separating births, survival and movement when interpreting changes in sightings. *A dog disappearing from observation is not always a death; the authors include human removal and dispersal. Full text checked.*
- **Reece et al. (2008), [Fecundity and longevity of roaming dogs in Jaipur, India](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-6)**. Uses ABC programme records and marked dogs to estimate reproductive and survival parameters. *Survival was inferred from recaptures of 62 spayed females, not all dogs.*
- **Yoak et al. (2014), [Disease control through fertility control: Secondary benefits of animal birth control in Indian street dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2013.09.005)**. Compares health measures across Rajasthan cities with different ABC histories. *A cross-city observational comparison cannot isolate ABC as the cause of differences.*
- **Evans et al. (2022), [Free-roaming dog population dynamics in Ranchi, India](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rvsc.2021.12.022)**. Re-sightings and campaign records help interpret turnover and the need for repeated vaccination. *Observed losses could reflect mortality or migration; the study could not separate them.*
- **Gill et al. (2022), [Stray Dogs and Public Health: Population Estimation in Punjab, India](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci9020075)**. Compares mark–re-sight estimators, useful when choosing how to extrapolate from a sighting survey. *Estimator outputs differ and sampling choices limit confidence in the statewide extrapolation.*
- **Gibson et al. (2022), [Elimination of human rabies in Goa, India through an integrated One Health approach](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-30371-y)**. State-scale mapped vaccination and surveillance work, relevant to repeat visits and recording which dogs have been reached. *The outcomes belong to a combined programme, not the isolated effect of vaccination, sterilisation or education.*

## Behaviour, welfare and people

- **Corfmat et al. (published online 2022), [Community attitudes and perceptions towards free-roaming dogs in Goa, India](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888705.2021.2014839)**. Household survey covering feeding, fear, conflict and attitudes to dogs remaining in communities. Useful for understanding why a sightings app must account for both carers and people afraid of dogs. *Household responses in Goa, not a national opinion poll. Abstract checked; full text not in our library.*
- **Banerjee et al. (2022), [Time-activity budget of urban-adapted free-ranging dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10211-021-00379-6)**. Observational work on what free-ranging dogs do through the day. Useful when sampling dog sightings at different times instead of treating absence from the map as absence from a place. *Sampling context matters. Full text in the Dognosis library.*
- **Bhattacharjee et al. (2017), [Free-ranging dogs show age related plasticity in their ability to follow human pointing](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0180643)**. Tests response to human pointing across ages. Useful context for human-dog interactions and the limits of behaviour inferred from a photograph. *An experimental interaction, not a welfare measure. Full text in the Dognosis library.*
- **Bhalla et al. (2021), [‘Stray appetites’: a socio-ecological analysis of free-ranging dogs living alongside human communities in Bangalore, India](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11252-021-01097-4)**. Brings together photo-based capture–recapture, food-source mapping and resident interviews in Bangalore, a close match to IndieDex's setting. *Associations between food and dog density cannot show how much a specific food-policy change would reduce numbers.*
- **Srinivasan et al. (2019), [Reorienting rabies research and practice: Lessons from India](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-019-0358-y)**. Field and archival work, principally in Chennai, on how everyday human-dog relationships shape rabies policy. *Interpretive research, not a trial of a dog-management intervention.*

## History and ways of framing the issue

- **Narayanan (2016), [Street dogs at the intersection of colonialism and informality: ‘Subaltern animism’ as a posthuman critique of Indian cities](https://doi.org/10.1177/0263775816672860)**. A historical and urban-theory argument about how Indian cities have treated street dogs. Useful background for the language and politics of “stray” versus “community” dogs. *An interpretive paper, not a population study. Publisher abstract checked; full text not reviewed.*
- **Kumar (2026), [The Indian street dog crisis and multispecies coexistence in tropical urban futures](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44284-026-00476-2)**. The paper shared in `#nammaindies` that prompted this list. A starting point for discussion of coexistence and urban policy. *Publisher page did not return readable full text here; do not treat this entry as an endorsement of specific findings until read.*

## Computer vision and individual re-identification

This is a practical starting point, not evidence that a model trained on other species or a kennel population works on India's street dogs. IndieDex currently uses YOLO26x to find animals and MiewID to rank candidate matches, then asks a human to confirm identity; its own [README](../README.md) and [agent guide](../AGENTS.md) describe measured limits. These papers suggest approaches to evaluate, not a validated Indian field re-ID accuracy:

- **Čermák et al. (2024), [WildlifeDatasets: An open-source toolkit for animal re-identification](https://doi.org/10.1109/WACV57701.2024.00585)**. Dataset and evaluation tooling for comparing individual-animal recognition methods; useful for building repeatable IndieDex re-ID tests. *Cross-species benchmarks do not establish performance on Indian street dogs.*
- **Cermak et al. (2025), [WildFusion: Individual Animal Identification with Calibrated Similarity Fusion](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-92387-6_2)**. Combines different image-similarity signals for animal identity ranking; potentially useful when a single embedding cannot separate look-alike dogs. *Test on session-separated street-dog photographs before adopting it; publisher full text was not reviewed here.*
- **Czupryna et al. (2023), [Testing novel facial recognition technology to identify dogs during vaccination campaigns](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49522-2)**. A field test of phone-based dog identification against microchips for vaccination follow-up. *Conducted in rural Tanzania, not India; operator-level sensitivity and photo conditions limit transfer to IndieDex.*

Before adding a model to the app, check its dataset, licence, and whether the evaluation separates photos from the same camera session.
